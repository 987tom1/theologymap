# Scripture

## Canon · T1 · certain
  hold  The canon includes the deuterocanonical books received by the church from the earliest centuries, read as Scripture in Christian worship long before the Reformation, and confirmed as canonical by ecumenical and local council.
  why   The books the church read as Scripture in its worship and drew doctrine from across the patristic period are the books the church holds as Scripture, and the sixteenth century is late to narrow a canon the church had already received.
  vs    Restricting the canon to the sixty-six books of the Hebrew canon plus the New Testament, treating the deuterocanonical books as edifying but not Scripture.
  refs  2 Pet 3:15-16

## Sufficiency of Scripture · T1 · certain
  hold  Scripture is the highest written authority, and is transmitted, canonised and rightly interpreted within the living tradition and teaching authority of the church, which does not add to Scripture but safeguards its right reading.
  why   The canon itself was recognised by the church, and a text that could only be recognised by a prior authoritative body cannot coherently be read as sufficient apart from that body's ongoing role in interpreting it.
  vs    Scripture alone as the sole binding authority, which treats the church's role in recognising and transmitting the canon as merely historical rather than as an authority that continues.
  refs  2 Thess 2:15; John 21:25
  link  clarity-of-scripture
  link  the-lords-supper

## Hermeneutic method · T2 · certain
  hold  Scripture is read in continuity with the church's received tradition of interpretation, including a legitimate use of typological and allegorical reading where the historic church has consistently used it, alongside attention to the literal sense.
  why   The literal sense is foundational, but the church fathers and the liturgical tradition read many texts typologically in ways later ages have found spiritually fruitful and doctrinally sound, and abandoning that reading in favour of a purely literal method discards a genuine mode of scriptural meaning.
  vs    Restricting legitimate meaning to what grammatical-historical method alone can establish, setting aside a use of typology and allegory the historic church regarded as a real, secondary sense of the text.

## Clarity of Scripture · T2.5 · certain
  hold  Scripture's meaning is safeguarded and authoritatively set forth through the church's teaching office, so that private reading is a genuine good but not a sufficient guarantee of rightly understanding what Scripture teaches.
  why   Scripture itself records serious disagreement among readers acting in good faith, and a text important enough to define salvation is one whose interpretation the church cannot leave to private judgment alone.
  vs    Treating personal or private reading as sufficient on its own to establish doctrine, without reference to the church's authoritative interpretation.
  refs  2 Pet 1:20; 2 Pet 3:16
  link  hermeneutic-method


# God

## Trinity · T1 · certain
  hold  God is one in being and three in person — Father, Son and Holy Spirit — eternally distinct, equal in nature and glory, and not three gods nor one person under three names.
  why   Scripture names Father, Son and Spirit together as God while insisting that God is one, and the creeds of 325 and 381 state that pattern in the only terms that keep both halves of it.
  vs    Accounts that secure the oneness of God by dissolving the distinction of the persons, or that secure the distinction by making the Son and the Spirit less than God.
  refs  Matt 28:19; John 1:1-3; John 10:30; 2 Cor 13:13
  link  eternal-generation-of-the-son
  link  efs-ess

## Eternal generation of the Son · T1.5 · certain
  hold  The Son is eternally begotten of the Father — receiving the one undivided divine essence from the Father without beginning, without division and without subordination of nature — and the Spirit eternally proceeds.
  why   It is how the creeds state the distinction of the persons without introducing degrees of deity: what distinguishes the Son from the Father is the manner of having the divine life, not the amount of it.
  vs    The view that eternal generation is a speculative inheritance from Greek philosophy which the biblical text does not require.
  refs  John 1:14; John 1:18; John 5:26; Heb 1:3
  link  trinity
  link  efs-ess

## Open theism · T1.5 · certain
  hold  God knows the whole of the future exhaustively and infallibly, including every free choice creatures will make, and nothing that comes to pass is a discovery to him.
  why   God declares the end from the beginning as the mark that distinguishes him from every idol, and prophecy of free human acts across centuries requires that those acts be known before they occur.
  vs    The view that the free future is not yet available to be known, which this position holds concedes to creatures a part of reality God does not command.
  refs  Isa 46:9-10; Ps 139:4; Isa 42:9
  link  classical-theism
  link  divine-foreknowledge
  link  gods-relation-to-time

## Classical theism · T2 · certain
  hold  God is simple, immutable, impassible and eternally self-sufficient — not composed of parts, not changing in being or purpose, and not moved from outside himself — so that his love and mercy are the fullness of his own life rather than states produced in him by the world.
  why   A God who could be added to or diminished would depend on what is not God, and the scriptures that speak of God relenting are read as accommodation to human ways of speaking, as the same books elsewhere say plainly that God does not change.
  vs    The view that God is genuinely affected and changed by what creatures do, which this position holds makes God one being among others rather than the source of all being.
  refs  Mal 3:6; Jas 1:17; Num 23:19; Exod 3:14
  link  gods-relation-to-time
  link  open-theism

## EFS / ESS · T2.5 · certain
  hold  The Son's obedience to the Father belongs to his incarnate mission and to the economy of salvation, and within the eternal life of God the three persons share one undivided will and one authority.
  why   The councils held that a distinct will implies a distinct nature, so an eternal relation of command and obedience between Father and Son would divide what Nicaea and Chalcedon confessed to be one.
  vs    An eternal ordering of authority and submission between Father and Son, which this position holds cannot be had without dividing the one divine will.
  refs  John 5:19; Phil 2:5-8; John 10:30
  link  trinity
  link  eternal-generation-of-the-son

## God's relation to time · T3 · confident
  hold  God exists outside the succession of moments altogether, in a single undivided present to which every point in created time is equally immediate, so that God does not wait, anticipate or remember.
  why   Time is a feature of the created order, and a God who is its maker is not one of its inhabitants; the classical account of eternity as the whole, simultaneous and perfect possession of unending life follows from that.
  vs    The view that God experiences succession, which this position holds would make God's life measurable by the creation he made.
  refs  Ps 90:2; Exod 3:14; 2 Pet 3:8
  link  classical-theism
  link  divine-foreknowledge

## Divine foreknowledge · T3 · confident
  hold  God simply knows the whole future as it will actually be, without that knowledge being grounded in a decree that determines it or in a prior knowledge of what creatures would do under other circumstances.
  why   Foreknowledge is knowledge, not causation, and a God who sees what will be does not thereby make it be; the traditions holding this read God's knowledge of the future as reading it rather than as fixing it.
  vs    Accounts that make God's knowledge of the future depend on his having determined it, which this position holds turns foreknowledge into another name for predestination.
  refs  Rom 8:29; 1 Pet 1:2; Isa 46:9-10
  link  open-theism
  link  classical-theism
  link  gods-relation-to-time


# Christ

## Deity and humanity · T1 · certain
  hold  Christ is one person subsisting in two complete natures, divine and human, united without confusion, change, division or separation, each nature retaining its own properties.
  why   This is the church's settled reading of a Christ who is worshipped as God and yet genuinely hungry, weary and tempted as man.
  vs    Any account that blends the two natures into one, divides Christ into two separate subjects, or diminishes either full deity or full humanity.
  refs  John 1:1,14; Col 2:9
  link  the-lords-supper

## Virgin birth · T1 · certain
  hold  Jesus was conceived in Mary's womb by the power of the Holy Spirit, without a human father, so that from the very beginning of his human existence he was both fully God and fully man.
  why   Matthew and Luke independently report a virginal conception, and the church has confessed it as a mark of the incarnation's supernatural origin since its earliest creeds.
  vs    Treating the virgin birth as a later legendary addition to an originally ordinary human birth narrative.
  refs  Matt 1:18-23; Luke 1:34-35
  link  deity-and-humanity

## Bodily resurrection · T1 · certain
  hold  Christ rose from the dead bodily and physically on the third day, the same body that was crucified now transformed and glorified, leaving the tomb empty and appearing to witnesses who touched him and watched him eat.
  why   The Gospels report an empty tomb and a body that could be touched and that ate food, and Paul grounds every believer's own future resurrection hope in the fact that Christ's was bodily.
  vs    Any account that reduces the resurrection to a purely spiritual experience, a vision, or a continuing memory or influence, without an empty tomb and a transformed physical body.
  refs  1 Cor 15:3-8; Luke 24:36-43
  link  deity-and-humanity

## Atonement · T1.5 · confident
  hold  Orthodox theology holds Christus Victor and recapitulation as one integrated account of the cross: Christ, as the new Adam, retraces and reverses humanity's fall and in so doing breaks the dominion of sin and death, rather than treating victory and recapitulation as two separate logics.
  refs  Rom 3:24-26; Col 2:14-15; 1 Cor 15:3; Isa 53:4-6

## Impeccability · T3 · confident
  hold  Because the human nature never exists or acts except in the person of the eternal Son, it was not possible for Christ to sin; the wilderness and Gethsemane temptations were genuinely felt by the human nature even though yielding to them was never a live possibility for the person.
  why   Sin belongs to a person and not a nature in the abstract, and the person of Christ is the eternal Son, who cannot sin; a Christ who could actually have sinned would not be a fully reliable Savior.
  vs    A Christ whose sinlessness was genuinely at risk and had to be achieved, which this view judges to be incompatible with the person acting being God the Son.
  refs  James 1:13


# Holy Spirit

## Sealing of the Spirit · T2 · certain
  hold  The Spirit's seal named in Ephesians is sacramentally sealed and made visible in confirmation, or chrismation, when the newly baptized are anointed and receive the gift of the Holy Spirit through the laying on of hands.
  why   The apostles laid hands on the newly baptized to give the Spirit, and the church's rite of confirmation or chrismation continues that apostolic act as the visible completion of the seal Scripture describes.
  vs    Treating the Spirit's seal as complete in an inward, unmediated gift at the moment of believing, without the church's sacramental act connected to the laying on of hands.
  refs  Eph 1:13-14; Acts 8:14-17
  link  baptism-in-the-holy-spirit
  link  baptism

## Healing · T2 · leaning
  hold  God really heals the sick today in answer to believing prayer, but does so as a sovereign gift given according to his own will rather than as an entitlement guaranteed to every believer who asks in faith.
  why   James instructs the elders to pray over the sick and anoint them with oil, promising that the prayer of faith will save the sick, while the rest of the New Testament's healings remain acts of God's free compassion rather than a formula.
  vs    Guaranteeing physical healing in this life as a provision of the atonement that faith can claim, so that continued sickness is read as a failure of faith.
  refs  James 5:14-16; 2 Cor 12:7-9

## Baptism in the Holy Spirit · T2.5 · certain
  hold  The fullness of the Holy Spirit given at conversion is sealed and completed in the sacrament of confirmation, or chrismation, administered through the laying on of hands and anointing with chrism.
  why   The apostles laid hands on the newly baptized so that they might receive the Holy Spirit, and the church continues this apostolic act as the sacrament that completes Christian initiation begun in baptism.
  vs    Locating the giving of the Spirit's fullness in an inward experience sought apart from the church's sacramental rite of confirmation or chrismation.
  refs  Acts 8:14-17; Acts 19:5-6
  link  baptism


# Humanity and sin

## Image of God · T1.5 · confident
  hold  The image of God includes the rational and free soul and the capacity for communion with God, understood together as the ground of humanity's calling toward likeness with God.
  refs  Gen 1:26-27; Gen 9:6; Col 3:10
  link  historical-adam

## Historical Adam · T2.5 · leaning
  hold  Adam and Eve were real historical individuals, specially created by God, and every other human being descends from them, so that Adam's fall is transmitted to the whole race by ordinary generation.
  why   Genesis narrates Adam's formation and Eve's formation from his side as events rather than literary devices, and Paul treats Adam as a single individual through whom sin and death entered the world for all.
  vs    Treating Adam as a real historical figure whose headship over the race did not require being every living human's only biological ancestor.
  refs  Gen 2:7; Gen 3:20; Rom 5:12
  link  original-sin

## Depravity and prevenient grace · T2.5 · certain
  hold  Grace initiates and accompanies the whole process of coming to faith, since no one can turn to God without it, but the person's own free cooperation with that grace, rather than a separate prior act of grace restoring ability, is part of what faith itself consists in.
  why   Neither an unaided human turning to God nor an account in which the will contributes nothing to the assent of faith is accepted here: grace and freedom are held to act together as a single, non-competing cause of a person's coming to believe.
  vs    Treating grace and human freedom as though enabling one required subtracting from the other, so either grace alone or the will alone must be given the whole credit for faith.
  refs  Phil 2:12-13
  link  original-sin

## Age of accountability · T2.5 · confident
  hold  The standing of infants and others incapable of personal faith is addressed through incorporation into the Church by baptism rather than through a threshold of moral accountability, and the fate of those who die unbaptized is entrusted to God's mercy without a settled dogmatic answer.
  why   Baptism, not a developmental stage, is the ordinary means by which a person, including an infant, is united to Christ and the church, so the pastoral question is framed around the sacrament rather than around when culpability might begin.
  vs    Answering the same pastoral question through a threshold of moral or cognitive capacity rather than through incorporation by baptism.
  refs  Acts 2:38-39
  link  baptism
  link  original-sin

## Original sin · T3 · certain
  hold  Through Adam's fall, mortality and a corrupted, sin-prone condition entered the human race and are inherited by every person, but Adam's personal guilt for his own transgression is not inherited or imputed to his descendants.
  why   Eastern theology reads Romans 5:12's phrase 'because all sinned' as each person's own actual sinning within a shared corrupted and mortal condition, and holds that guilt, a property of a personal act, cannot be transmitted the way mortality and corruption are.
  vs    Holding that Adam's own guilt, and not only the corrupted and mortal condition his sin produced, is imputed to every descendant at birth.
  refs  Rom 5:12; Ezek 18:20
  link  historical-adam
  link  age-of-accountability
  link  baptism

## Dichotomy vs trichotomy · T3 · confident
  hold  Human nature consists of two constitutive parts, a material body and a single immaterial aspect that Scripture calls, interchangeably, the soul or the spirit.
  why   Scripture regularly uses soul and spirit as parallel or interchangeable terms for the same immaterial reality, as when Jesus commits his spirit to the Father at death while other texts speak of the soul departing.
  vs    Treating soul and spirit as two separate constitutive parts of a person rather than two names for one immaterial aspect.
  refs  Luke 23:46; Eccl 12:7


# Salvation

## Justification · T1 · certain
  hold  Salvation is participation in the life of God, a real union with Christ that transfigures the believer by grace, and the West's forensic categories of imputed or infused righteousness are not the terms in which this participation is chiefly described.
  why   Peter writes of becoming partakers of the divine nature, and the Eastern fathers describe salvation as theosis, a real deification by grace that neither reduces to a legal verdict nor separates declaration from transformation.
  vs    Organizing salvation around the forensic question of how righteousness is reckoned, a framework this position holds is a specifically Western development rather than the church's original vocabulary.
  refs  2 Pet 1:4; John 17:20-23
  link  original-sin
  link  depravity-and-prevenient-grace
  link  regeneration-and-baptism
  link  assurance

## Sovereignty and free will · T2.5 · certain
  hold  Salvation unfolds through synergy, divine grace and human free will cooperating in the lifelong work of theosis, a framework that does not divide along the West's sovereignty-versus-freedom debate.
  why   The Eastern fathers describe salvation as cooperation between divine grace and human will from the outset, never posing grace and freedom as competitors that must be balanced against each other.
  vs    Framing the question as a choice between monergism and libertarian freedom, categories this reading regards as belonging to a specifically Western debate rather than to the church's original vocabulary.
  refs  2 Pet 1:4; Phil 2:12-13
  link  perseverance-and-apostasy
  link  depravity-and-prevenient-grace
  link  divine-foreknowledge

## Regeneration and baptism · T2.5 · certain
  hold  God ordinarily gives the new birth through the washing of water baptism itself, so that baptism is the instrument, not merely the sign, of the regeneration it accompanies.
  why   Titus speaks of a washing of regeneration, and Peter writes that baptism now saves, language read as attaching new birth to the washing itself rather than to a prior, separate experience.
  vs    Locating the new birth entirely in an inward experience prior to and apart from water baptism, so the water rite becomes a testimony to a birth already complete rather than its ordinary means.
  refs  Titus 3:5; John 3:5; 1 Pet 3:21
  link  baptism
  link  justification
  link  original-sin

## Perseverance and apostasy · T3 · certain
  hold  Grave, deliberate sin against a known command can destroy the sanctifying grace given in justification, but that grace, once lost, can be restored through repentance and confession rather than being finally and irrecoverably forfeited.
  why   Grave, deliberate sin against a known command is held to destroy the grace given in justification rather than merely to wound it, and the church's ordinary provision for that loss is repentance and confession, so grace once lost is restored through the church's own remedy rather than by a wholly new justification.
  vs    Treating salvation as either unlosable regardless of subsequent sin or as finally forfeited beyond any provision for restoration, both of which this position holds misdescribe how grace, once given, relates to serious sin and repentance.
  refs  1 John 5:16-17; James 5:19-20; Gal 5:4
  link  assurance
  link  sovereignty-and-free-will
  link  justification

## Assurance · T3 · certain
  hold  A believer may know present grace and hope in God's mercy, but a settled certainty of final salvation ahead of death is not sought or promised in this life, so as to guard against presumption while sanctifying grace remains at stake.
  why   What is promised is a well-founded hope in God's mercy rather than an infallible certainty of one's own final perseverance, so the counsel is to rest in present grace and guard against presumption while the outcome of a life still lies ahead.
  vs    Claiming an infallible certainty of final salvation ahead of death apart from special revelation, which this position holds Scripture nowhere promises and which risks presumption.
  refs  Phil 2:12; 1 Cor 9:27; 2 Cor 5:10
  link  perseverance-and-apostasy
  link  justification


# Church

## Baptism · T2 · certain
  hold  Baptism is the instrument by which God gives new birth, forgiveness and union with Christ, and is therefore administered to infants and converts alike.
  why   The New Testament attaches the gift itself to the washing — new birth of water and Spirit, baptism that now saves — rather than to a sign of it.
  vs    Treating baptism as a sign of a grace given elsewhere, which detaches the promise from the act the New Testament attaches it to.
  refs  John 3:5; Titus 3:5; 1 Pet 3:21; Acts 22:16
  link  the-lords-supper
  link  membership-and-discipline

## The Lord's Supper · T2 · certain
  hold  The bread and wine are changed into the body and blood of Christ, truly and really, while the manner of the change is confessed as a mystery rather than defined in the scholastic vocabulary of substance and accident.
  why   Dositheus confesses the change of the elements in the plainest terms the East has ever used, and the same tradition has declined to make the Latin philosophical explanation of that change a term of communion.
  refs  1 Cor 11:23-26; John 6:53-58; Luke 22:19-20
  link  baptism

## Church government · T3 · certain
  hold  Authority is exercised through bishops standing in historic succession, who ordain clergy and oversee multiple congregations within a diocese.
  why   The New Testament pattern of apostolic oversight passed to Timothy and Titus as individuals charged with appointing elders, and the earliest post-apostolic churches were governed by a single bishop in each city.
  vs    Government by a plurality of elders with no single office standing over the local eldership.
  refs  1 Tim 3:1-7; Titus 1:5; 1 Tim 5:22
  link  membership-and-discipline

## Membership and discipline · T3 · certain
  hold  Belonging is constituted by baptism and sacramental standing within a parish or diocese, administered by the clergy under the bishop, rather than by a local covenant the congregation itself enters into.
  why   The church's boundary has historically been the sacraments themselves — baptism admits, and excommunication from the sacraments, exercised by the clergy, is the discipline the New Testament describes.
  vs    Requiring a separate congregational covenant beyond baptism and sacramental standing to establish who belongs.
  refs  1 Cor 5:1-13; Matt 18:15-17
  link  baptism
  link  church-government


# Last things

## Second coming · T1 · certain
  hold  Jesus Christ will return personally, bodily and visibly to judge the living and the dead and to bring his kingdom to its consummation, as the Nicene Creed confesses.
  why   The New Testament repeatedly promises Christ's own return in the same manner he departed, and the church has confessed exactly this since the fourth century without significant dissent.
  refs  Acts 1:11; 1 Thess 4:16-17
  link  millennium
  link  rapture

## New creation · T2 · confident
  hold  The present creation itself will be liberated, purified and transformed into the new heavens and new earth, in continuity with the world that exists now rather than its replacement by an unrelated new one.
  why   Creation is described as groaning in anticipation of being set free rather than annihilated, and the resurrection of the body, itself a transformation of what already exists rather than a wholly new creation from nothing, is the pattern for creation's own renewal.
  vs    Reading the present creation as destroyed and replaced rather than purified and transformed.
  refs  Rom 8:19-21; 1 Cor 15:42-44
  link  second-coming
  link  hell

## Millennium · T3 · confident
  hold  The thousand years of Revelation 20 is a symbolic description of the whole present age between Christ's first and second comings, during which the church shares his reign and Satan is restrained from deceiving the nations, ending in one resurrection and judgment at Christ's return.
  why   Revelation's numbers are consistently symbolic elsewhere in the book, and the New Testament elsewhere describes the kingdom as already inaugurated in Christ's reign rather than confined to a future earthly period.
  vs    Placing the thousand-year reign in the future, either as an earthly reign beginning at Christ's return or a golden age preceding it.
  refs  Rev 20:1-6; Col 1:13
  link  second-coming
  link  rapture

## Hell · T3 · certain
  hold  The unrepentant are raised bodily and consciously suffer just punishment for sin forever, a state as everlasting as the life of the redeemed.
  why   The same Greek word for 'eternal' describes both the punishment of the wicked and the life of the righteous in the same verse, and Revelation describes the suffering of the condemned as continuing day and night forever and ever.
  vs    Holding that the wicked cease to exist after judgment rather than suffering consciously without end, or that judgment is ultimately restorative rather than final.
  refs  Matt 25:46; Rev 14:11
  link  second-coming
  link  intermediate-state

## Intermediate state · T3 · confident
  hold  The soul's journey after death, prayed for and remembered by the Church, still awaits the final judgment and resurrection; the Church continues to pray and offer the Divine Liturgy for the departed, trusting that such prayer benefits them, without defining that interval as a formal process of expiatory punishment.
  why   The Church's ancient and continuous practice of praying and offering the Liturgy for the departed presumes the prayers of the living genuinely aid the dead, while the Church has never dogmatically defined the toll-house imagery found in some ascetical writings as binding doctrine.
  vs    Defining the interval after death as a formal, dogmatically specified process of purification, as in the Catholic doctrine of purgatory.
  refs  2 Tim 1:16-18
  link  hell
  link  second-coming

## Rapture · T4 · certain
  hold  The meeting in the air described in 1 Thessalonians 4 is the same event as Christ's visible second coming, not a separate prior rapture, so that the resurrection and gathering of believers happen at the one, single return of Christ.
  why   1 Thessalonians 4 and Matthew 24 both use the same picture of gathering the elect at Christ's coming, and the New Testament nowhere clearly presents two separate future comings of Christ rather than one.
  vs    Distinguishing a rapture of the church from Christ's visible return as two separate future events.
  refs  1 Thess 4:16-17; Matt 24:29-31
  link  millennium
  link  second-coming


# Creation and science

## Creation ex nihilo and design · T1.5 · certain
  hold  God created the universe out of nothing, by a free act of his will, not from pre-existing matter and not by any necessity in his own nature.
  why   Scripture presents God speaking the universe into being and calling into existence the things that do not exist, and the historic creeds confess him as maker of all things visible and invisible.
  vs    Treating matter as eternal and uncreated, or treating the universe as an emanation flowing of necessity from the divine nature rather than as a free act of will.
  refs  Gen 1:1; John 1:3; Col 1:16; Heb 11:3
  link  classical-theism
  link  genre-of-genesis-1

## Miracles and science · T2 · certain
  hold  Miracles are real, particular acts of God within his ordinarily ordered creation, neither violations of a self-sufficient nature nor beyond the reach of a God who freely sustains the natural order he made.
  why   Scripture presents God's miracles, culminating in the bodily resurrection of Christ, as real events within history and nature, consistent with a God who freely governs the order he continually sustains rather than a deity bound by it.
  vs    Treating the miraculous as either scientifically impossible in principle or as merely a symbolic idiom for events that did not actually occur.
  refs  John 2:11; Acts 2:22; 1 Cor 15:14-17
  link  classical-theism
  link  trinity

## Genre of Genesis 1 · T3 · leaning
  hold  Genesis 1 is straightforward historical narrative, describing God's creation of the universe and all it contains across six ordinary, sequential days.
  why   The chapter uses ordinary Hebrew narrative verb forms and numbers each day with the formula evening and morning, the same construction Exodus later treats as the pattern for the ordinary work week.
  vs    Reading the days of Genesis 1 as a topical framework, a long-age analogy, or a temple-inauguration account rather than as an ordinary sequence of days.
  refs  Gen 1:1-31; Exod 20:11
  link  hermeneutic-method


# The unseen realm

## Angels and demons · T2 · certain
  hold  Angels and demons are real created spiritual beings, Satan and demons among them fallen through rebellion against God, resisted through ordinary prayer, Scripture, and the sacraments rather than through heightened attention or specialised practice.
  why   The historic confessions affirm a spiritual realm of angels and fallen angels but locate the Christian's resistance to evil within ordinary practice rather than a specialised discipline.
  vs    Treating angels and demons as a subject requiring specialised attention, heightened vigilance, or dedicated ministry beyond the ordinary means of grace.
  refs  Col 1:16; Jude 1:6
  link  deliverance-ministry
  link  the-creeds

## Deliverance ministry · T2.5 · certain
  hold  Deliverance from demonic affliction is exercised through the church's formal rite of exorcism, administered only by a priest specifically authorised by the bishop after careful discernment, never as private or freelance practice.
  why   The church holds authority over demons as a body, not as scattered individuals, so the rite is reserved to ordained ministers under episcopal oversight to guard against both presumption and error.
  vs    Treating deliverance as a ministry any believer may exercise informally, without ordination or the bishop's specific authorisation.
  refs  Mark 16:17; James 5:14-16
  link  angels-and-demons
  link  healing


# Ethics

## Marriage and sexuality · T2 · certain
  hold  Marriage is a covenant between one man and one woman, instituted by God in creation and reaffirmed by Christ, and sexual intimacy belongs within that covenant alone.
  why   Scripture presents marriage as the one-flesh union of a man and a woman from creation onward, a pattern Christ himself reaffirms as the standard rather than one option among others, and channels sexual desire toward covenant fidelity within it.
  vs    Extending marriage, and the blessing of sexual union within it, to same-sex couples, on the ground that Scripture's creation pattern does not itself settle that question.
  refs  Gen 2:24; Matt 19:4-6; 1 Cor 7:2
  link  divorce-and-remarriage

## Abortion · T2 · certain
  hold  Unborn human life possesses full moral status from conception, so intentionally taking that life is a grave moral wrong; where a mother's life is genuinely threatened, treating the underlying medical crisis is distinguished from directly ending the pregnancy.
  why   Scripture describes the unborn as already known and formed by God in the womb, using the same personal language it uses for those already born, giving no warrant for a lesser moral status before birth.
  vs    Treating fetal life as acquiring full moral status only at some later point in development, or as a matter left to a mother's choice.
  refs  Ps 139:13-16; Luke 1:41-44
  link  ivf-and-embryos
  link  euthanasia

## War and violence · T2.5 · confident
  hold  War can be morally justified, and at times obligatory, when it meets criteria such as just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, last resort and proportionality, and Christians may serve as soldiers and bear the sword on the state's behalf.
  why   Civil government is given the sword by God to restrain evil and protect the innocent, and a Christian who serves within a just cause and legitimate authority exercises rather than abandons love of neighbor.
  vs    Holding that Christ's teaching closes the use of lethal force to Christians altogether, when this position holds Romans 13's grant of the sword to civil government extends to a Christian's own service within it.
  refs  Rom 13:1-4; Luke 3:14

## Divorce and remarriage · T3 · certain
  hold  Marriage is ordinarily indissoluble, but the church, exercising pastoral economy for human weakness, may permit a second or third marriage through a penitential rite once a marriage has effectively died through adultery or desertion.
  why   The church's pastoral tradition, reaching back to the fathers, treats the death of a marriage through adultery as a real rupture Christ's own exception names, and applies economy rather than strict application of the letter of the law to a broken and repentant situation.
  vs    Treating the church's pastoral economy toward a marriage's death either as a Catholic-style finding that no marriage existed or as an unqualified license to remarry without repentance.
  refs  Matt 19:9; Matt 5:32
  link  marriage-and-sexuality

## IVF and embryos · T3 · certain
  hold  In vitro fertilization is not permissible, because it separates the conception of a child from the marital act and because its standard practice creates embryonic human persons who are then routinely discarded, indefinitely frozen or destroyed in selection.
  why   Human life begins at fertilization regardless of where it occurs, so any procedure whose ordinary practice results in the loss of embryonic human persons is a grave wrong, and the marital act itself, not a laboratory procedure, is the fitting context for conceiving a child.
  vs    Treating in vitro fertilization as morally acceptable so long as embryos are not deliberately destroyed, when its ordinary practice still separates conception from the marital act and routinely results in surplus embryos.
  refs  Ps 139:13-16
  link  abortion

## Euthanasia · T3 · certain
  hold  Intentionally ending a patient's life is never permitted, but no one is obliged to pursue every possible intervention; disproportionate treatment may be withheld and death allowed to come, and pain relief that indirectly hastens it is distinguished from an act that intends it.
  why   Preserving life is a duty, not an absolute to be pursued by any means whatever the burden, so the historic distinction between ordinary and extraordinary care lets a dying person be allowed to die without that being treated as an act of killing.
  vs    Treating every possible means of prolonging life as obligatory regardless of its burden or proportion, so that withdrawing disproportionate treatment is itself judged wrong.
  refs  Eccl 3:1-2
  link  abortion

## Alcohol · T3 · confident
  hold  Scripture nowhere prohibits alcohol itself and repeatedly commends wine as a gift of God, so consumption in moderation is permitted while drunkenness is condemned as a sin.
  why   Wine is used in Scripture as an image of blessing and is present at the wedding at Cana and in the Lord's Supper itself, so the consistent target of biblical warning is drunkenness, not moderate use as such.
  vs    Treating any use of alcohol at all, rather than drunkenness specifically, as the sin Scripture warns against.
  refs  Ps 104:14-15; John 2:1-10

# Formation and practice

## Sanctification — progressive · T2 · certain
  hold  The Christian life is a lifelong, synergistic ascent into ever-deepening participation in God's own uncreated energies, a real sharing in the divine life that the church calls deification, worked out through ascetic struggle, the sacraments and unceasing prayer.
  why   Peter writes that believers are given what is needed to become partakers of the divine nature, and the church has always read growth in holiness as this real participation in God's own life rather than merely as improved moral conduct.
  vs    Confining the aim of the Christian life to moral improvement or the resolution of guilt, when Scripture and the fathers describe a real participation in the divine nature itself.
  refs  2 Pet 1:4; 2 Cor 3:18
  link  spiritual-disciplines
  link  depravity-and-prevenient-grace
  link  original-sin

## Suffering and providence · T2 · certain
  hold  God upholds and governs all things in his general providence and can redeem any suffering for good, but particular instances of suffering are permitted within that governance rather than specifically willed by God for their own sake.
  why   Scripture distinguishes what God does from what he allows, and holding suffering as permitted rather than specifically willed protects the goodness of God's character while still trusting his overarching governance of history.
  vs    Attributing a particular instance of suffering directly to God's specific intention, as though it were something he purposed for its own sake rather than something his governance permits and can redeem.
  refs  Job 1:12; 2 Cor 12:7-9
  link  divine-foreknowledge

## Spiritual disciplines · T3 · certain
  hold  The Christian life is formed by praying the church's daily office at fixed hours and by keeping the church year's cycle of fasts, feasts and seasons, so that the whole community is shaped by a shared, inherited rhythm rather than by private discretion.
  why   The church has prayed the psalter at fixed hours since the earliest centuries, and a shared calendar disciplines the whole body together rather than leaving formation to each believer's own initiative.
  vs    Leaving the pattern and timing of devotional practice to each believer's private discretion, with no shared daily office or church calendar to hold the community to a common rhythm.
  refs  Ps 119:164; Acts 3:1

# Missions and world religions

## Exclusivity of Christ · T1 · certain
  hold  Salvation is found only in Jesus Christ, who alone is the one mediator between God and humanity and the name by which anyone must be saved.
  why   Peter tells the Sanhedrin there is salvation in no one else, and Paul names one mediator between God and humanity, the man Christ Jesus, without qualification or exception.
  vs    Treating the world's religions as equally valid paths to God alongside faith in Christ.
  refs  Acts 4:12; 1 Tim 2:5; John 14:6

  link  trinity

## The unevangelised · T3 · confident
  hold  Christ is the only saviour, but his atoning work can be applied to those who, without ever hearing the gospel, respond in sincere faith and love to God as he has made himself known to them through creation and conscience.
  why   Those who through no fault of their own do not know the gospel of Christ but sincerely seek God, and try to do his will as they know it through the dictates of conscience, can achieve eternal salvation, since it is Christ who enlightens all who are willing to arrive at the truth.
  vs    Requiring explicit, conscious faith in the name of Jesus Christ as a condition of the salvation his death secured.
  refs  Rom 2:14-16; John 1:9

# Church history and authority

## The creeds · T1 · certain
  hold  The Nicene Creed is recited in its original conciliar text, confessing the Holy Spirit as proceeding from the Father, without the later Western filioque addition.
  why   The creed was fixed by ecumenical council, and no local church, including Rome, has the authority to alter its wording unilaterally, whatever the merits of the underlying theological question.
  vs    Adding the filioque clause to the creed's conciliar text on the authority of a single patriarchate rather than another ecumenical council.

  link  the-fathers

## Prima scriptura · T1.5 · certain
  hold  Scripture is read within Holy Tradition, the whole living faith of the church as carried by the seven ecumenical councils, the Fathers and the church's liturgical and sacramental life; Scripture is not read as a source standing apart from that life.
  why   The Bible itself is one expression of Holy Tradition, given to and preserved by the church, so reading it rightly means reading it as the church has always read it rather than as an isolated text.
  vs    Isolating Scripture from the church's continuous liturgical, conciliar and patristic life as though it could be rightly read apart from that context.
  refs  2 Thess 2:15

## Reformation solas · T1.5 · certain
  hold  Salvation is a work of grace from beginning to end, worked out through the human person's free, grace-enabled cooperation in a lifelong movement toward union with God, rather than framed through the Western Reformation's own categories of forensic justification.
  why   The New Testament and the Fathers describe salvation as healing and deification rather than primarily as a legal verdict, so the Reformation's own terms are not the terms in which the question was first asked.
  vs    Framing salvation primarily as a single forensic verdict received by faith apart from the lifelong, grace-enabled process of transformation the Fathers describe.
  refs  Eph 2:8-9; Rom 3:28; Rom 1:17

## The Fathers · T2.5 · certain
  hold  The universal consent of the Fathers is the organ through which the church discerns Holy Tradition, carrying an authority close to that of the councils whose faith it expresses.
  why   The Fathers are not private theologians but the church itself speaking through its saints and teachers, and their consensus is how the church has always recognised the boundaries of orthodox belief.
  vs    Reading Scripture as an isolated modern interpreter without reference to how the church has continuously understood it since the apostles.

## Roman Catholics and Orthodox · T3 · confident
  hold  Roman Catholic and Orthodox Christians belong to churches that possess the marks of a true church, valid sacraments and apostolic succession among them, standing in imperfect rather than full communion with other such churches.
  why   Where the essential marks of a true church are present, including valid baptism, apostolic order and a shared confession of Christ, the division between churches is a wound within the one church rather than a division between a true church and a false one.
  vs    Regarding a church lacking full communion as either not a real church at all, or as no different from any other body sharing the same doctrinal errors.
  refs  Eph 4:4-6; John 17:20-21
